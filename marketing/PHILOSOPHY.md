# Interval Brutalism

**A design philosophy for work measured in seconds.**

There is a particular kind of honesty that only appears under a clock. When the
interval starts, nothing is negotiable — not your mood, not your excuses, not
the number counting down. This movement takes that condition and makes it
visual. The work is austere because the subject is austere. Nothing decorative
survives forty seconds of effort, so nothing decorative survives on the canvas
either. Every mark earns its place or it is removed, and the removal is the
labour: what remains looks inevitable only because so much was taken away.

**Space and form.** Black is not a background here; it is the room the work
happens in. Composition proceeds by division — a field split by a single rule,
a horizon of tick marks, a block of white that arrives with the weight of a
bell. The grid is strict and visible in its consequences rather than its lines.
Elements are aligned to fractions of the canvas that repeat across every piece
in the series, so a viewer who sees three of them begins to feel the underlying
system without ever being shown it. That coherence is not a template. It is the
product of measuring the same intervals again and again until the proportions
stop arguing.

**Colour and material.** One value system: absolute black, absolute white, and
the narrow grey corridor between them. Colour is refused on principle, because
the moment a hue enters, the eye reads mood instead of fact. Greys carry
hierarchy — the dimmest text is not decoration but the quietest true thing on
the page. Surfaces are flat and unlit. No gradients pretending at depth, no
shadows pretending at objects. The material honesty is the point: ink on a
lightless field, meticulously calibrated so that the whites feel struck rather
than printed.

**Scale and rhythm.** Typography behaves like an interval: long stretches of
restraint punctuated by a single overwhelming gesture. One phrase may occupy a
third of the canvas while everything around it shrinks to a whisper of tracked
capitals. The rhythm is 40 on, 20 off — dense mark, generous rest, dense mark —
and it repeats down the composition until the eye is breathing in time with it.
Monospaced numerals appear as instrumentation, aligned to a common baseline the
way readings are aligned on a chart. Nothing is centred by default; things are
centred only when centring is the argument.

**Composition and hierarchy.** Each piece states one thing. A viewer moving at
the speed of a thumb should receive the whole idea before deciding whether to
stop, and a viewer who does stop should find a second layer — a tick pattern
that turns out to be a session timeline, a rule that turns out to mark a rest.
This double reading is what separates work made in an afternoon from work made
by someone at the top of their field: the surface is immediate, and underneath
it everything has been reasoned about. Margins are enormous and inviolable.
Nothing touches an edge. Nothing overlaps. The generosity of the empty space is
the evidence of care.

**On craftsmanship.** This work must look laboured over, because it was. The
tracking on every line of capitals is set by hand and by eye. The optical
centre sits above the mathematical centre, as it always should. Baselines
agree across pieces that will never be seen together. These are decisions
nobody will consciously notice and everybody will feel, and attending to them
is the entire discipline. The finished artefact should carry the quiet
authority of an instrument panel — something built to be read correctly under
pressure, by someone who has done this for years.
